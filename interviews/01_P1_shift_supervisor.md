## Transcript

**INT:** Thanks for making time. Can you walk me through a normal day, starting from when you wake up?

**P1:** Sure. I'm up at 4:40, that's the alarm I hate most. I have to be in at 6:15 for the early shift. The thing about my job is I don't have a normal day — I'm on a rotation. Early, middle, late, and then four days off. So a week is early, middle, late, and then I get to be a person for four days.

**INT:** Say "early" out loud for me.

**P1:** Early is 6:15 to 2:15. Middle is 2:15 to 10:15. Late is 10:15 to 6:15 in the morning, so I'd be on the floor at midnight, which is not where I want to be.

**INT:** What about the medications, where do those fit?

**P1:** Three of them are morning. They're all in a little box, the plastic kind with the days of the week. I have a pill box, the pharmacy gives you one. And then there are two that I only take sometimes — one for when my back is bad, and one that's a strong one for the pain, but I'm not supposed to take that one every day. So five in total in the box but only three every morning.

**INT:** So the box has five compartments, but only three get used on a regular morning.

**P1:** Right, exactly. And the two extra ones are in the compartment that says "as needed," which is the one I can never find in the morning. That's a real problem. I'm not going to sit there and read seven little labels with the lights on. It's obvious in the dark but I don't take them in the dark.

**INT:** What about a phone? Don't you check it in the morning?

**P1:** I do, but I don't look at it right away. There's a lot on it. I've got notifications from everything — my daughter, the group chat for work, my wife, and there's a flood of things I don't care about. I swipe through and I'm looking for messages. I don't go looking for my pills. That's the thing. Nothing says "pills." Everything else on my phone is more interesting than my pills.

**INT:** Have you ever used an app for this?

**P1:** I tried one. My sister downloaded it for me. It was a very nice app, I must admit. It told me every day, and then I missed one because I was on nights, and the next morning it was a big red thing. A big red circle. And I felt terrible. Like I'd done something wrong. I deleted it.

**INT:** What was the red circle for?

**P1:** For missing. That's all. It didn't ask me anything. It didn't say "you're on a night shift, want to move this?" It just told me I'd failed.

**INT:** When you say you felt terrible — say more about that.

**P1:** I have done everything right for that medication for two years and one screen tells me I'm a bad patient. I don't need that. I'd rather have nothing. I'd rather not know than be told off.

**INT:** So a blank day feels better than a red one?

**P1:** Yes. And I'd rather have nothing than something that shames me. That's a strong sentence but it's true. I think about that app and I get a bit angry.

**INT:** What would a good version look like? Not a feature list, just what it would feel like.

**P1:** One page. When I open it, I see the three for today and I press a big button. Done. I want it to know that tonight I'm on nights, not to lecture me. Tomorrow's problem is tomorrow's.

**INT:** Tomorrow's problem is tomorrow's. I like that.

**P1:** It's on my wall. It's a poster. Well, it's on my fridge.

**INT:** Anything on your phone you can read comfortably? Anything you struggle with?

**P1:** The font sizes are small and I'm not a young person. I have glasses for reading and I take them off to read the phone, which is not convenient when my hands are full. Also I'm on the floor a lot, so it's one hand, and the phone is in a case already. My hands aren't great by the end of a shift, I should be honest about that. Buttons need to be big. I've pressed the wrong thing before.

**INT:** Tell me about the wrong thing.

**P1:** I pressed the "snooze" and it disappeared. It just vanished and I thought — has it gone? Is it recorded? I couldn't tell. That's a horrible feeling. If something disappears I don't know about, I can't trust it.

**INT:** If you press the same button twice, is that a problem?

**P1:** If I press it twice I want the day to still be counted once. Because I'll do that — I'll forget that I did it and press it again. That's not a problem, that's just me. As long as it stays "taken."

**INT:** How do you feel about a family member seeing your record?

**P1:** My daughter Sofia checks on me, and I love her for it. But I don't want her seeing everything — like if I have a bad day I don't want a report. I'd want to choose what she sees. She's a nurse, so she'll use anything against me. "The app says you missed two." I don't want that in my house. So it has to be my choice what leaves the phone. It has to be my choice.

**INT:** Side effects — you mentioned something last time?

**P1:** The blood pressure one. When I started it I felt dizzy for about two weeks, and I thought I'd made a mistake, I'd been given the wrong thing. Nobody explained it, or I wasn't listening. And I very nearly stopped taking it. That's the danger, isn't it? The side effect makes you stop the thing that's helping you.

**INT:** Would you want the app to explain that?

**P1:** I would want it to say "this is normal, it settles, here's when to call." I would want it written by a person, not a robot. A robot telling me about my body is not something I trust.

**INT:** Refills.

**P1:** That's the thing that sneaks up on me. I'm not a hoarder, I don't want a month of boxes in the house. And the pharmacy calls me — well, they text me — and they say it's ready, and by the time I get there it's sometimes gone, or it's on hold. Last month I had two days left and they hadn't got it, and I had to ring around. That stress is worse than the actual taking of the pills.

**INT:** Anything you'd want to be able to check? Just information about the medication itself.

**P1:** What is this pill for? What happens if I miss it? What should I not take it with? What does the side effect mean, and is it serious, or is it just me being dramatic? Half the leaflets are written for doctors. Nobody wrote them for me.

**INT:** Anything about your box — the physical one?

**P1:** I lost it. That's the truth. I lost the actual box. It was in a coat pocket, and the coat went to my friend's, and it never came back. There's a spare in the drawer with old ones in it, so it's not catastrophic, but that tells you how confident I am.

**INT:** Can you estimate — without it being a number you have to defend — how many doses you'd say you miss?

**P1:** It depends which week. If I'm on days off it's rare. If I've just come off nights, I would say one or two. Maybe. It's not something I count. My daughter would say I miss more than I say I miss.

**INT:** Last question. How old are you? The form said 58.

**P1:** (laughs) Fifty-eight. I keep it on the form and I update it, I just — hold on, I turned 59 in June. I only wrote it last year when I set the form up and forgot to change it. So 59. I've been 59 for three months. Does that ruin everything?

**INT:** Not at all. Thank you for correcting that — I'm writing down both answers.

**P1:** You write those down, the wrong ones?

**INT:** The wrong ones are the useful ones. A wrong number in an app is how people get told off for no reason.

**P1:** That's exactly it. [laughs]

## Analyst notes (not verbatim, added by interviewer)

- Age conflict: screener 58, self-corrected to 59. Do not silently pick one.
- Strong negative emotion attached to a red "missed" state. Deletion of a perfectly good app is a behaviour, not an opinion.
- Privacy need is about *granularity of sharing*, not about hiding everything. She wants to choose, and she names her daughter by name.
- The pill box is still in use. An app that ignores the physical routine will be an app that loses.
