## Transcript

**INT:** You said in the form that you read privacy policies. Talk to me about that.

**P5:** Ha, well, I read them because I'm suspicious, not because I'm interesting. I read them so I know what a company can do with the fact that I take a psychiatric medication. That's the sensitive one. Sertraline — I'll say it out loud, it's not a secret — but the fact that I'm on it is not something I want in a dataset. I read the policy to check that they don't sell it, and they don't, so fine. But I know the terms, and I know what "improving the service" can mean in a policy.

**INT:** What's your setup like?

**P5:** Two in the morning, both of them, at breakfast. One is the thyroid one which has to be at the same time every day — same time, not "morning" — because the thyroid one punishes you if you wander. And the sertraline, which is also morning. The migraine one is as needed, and I get maybe two a month. So: two boxes, a fixed pair in the morning, one loose in a drawer.

**INT:** And a "same time every day" ritual for the thyroid one?

**P5:** Same time means I have an actual relationship with it. It's at 07:20. My coffee is at 07:25, so the pill goes in and the coffee follows. Five minutes earlier is a different day as far as my thyroid is concerned, apparently. The one *before* food, they should say which. Anyway.

**INT:** How do you actually keep it?

**P5:** Badly, and in a way I'd defend. I have a phone reminder that I have never once dismissed, and it repeats every twenty minutes until I swipe it, so it becomes a background hum rather than an event. And I have a weekly box, which is genuinely the thing that works. The reminder is a backup. The box is the plan. If the box works, the reminder doesn't matter. The reminder exists for the day the box doesn't happen — a weekend away, a flight, someone staying over.

**INT:** Say more about that day.

**P5:** Right, the box is a *home* system. The box is only correct in my kitchen. On a Friday I'm in someone else's kitchen and the box says Wednesday, and Wednesday is a lie. The box does not travel. And on those days my adherence is a mess, and I'd rather it wasn't. That's the actual failure mode, and nobody designs for it because everyone's home is the same as their pill box.

**INT:** How do you handle those days now?

**P5:** Badly and manually. I put the two in a bag with my passport, which I do not recommend, because if the bag is in the washroom on a Friday night, that's it. I check the bag, but the bag doesn't check me. And last year I got the thyroid wrong on a Thursday in another country, and the next morning I felt like someone had put sand in my brain, and it took four days to come back. Four days. It's not dangerous, it's just — I couldn't read a sentence. That's what happens: you don't feel sick, you feel *stupid*, and you'd assume you were just tired.

**INT:** Would you want an app to explain that?

**P5:** I already know that, because I've read about it. That's the difference between me and the person who hasn't. If you're building for the person who hasn't read about it, then fine, tell them. If you're building for me, I don't want to be managed. There's a very specific failure in health apps where the product treats you like a patient who happens to be competent in a different area. "Hi, glad you're here! Let's get your first dose logged!" God.

**INT:** You have strong feelings about onboarding.

**P5:** Strong feelings. My first thirty seconds with a health app should be: I already know what I'm supposed to do, tell me something I don't know. That's the deal. If you spend my first minute asking me things I can already answer, I know exactly what the product is, and it's a form, and the thing I need is not a form, it's the three sentences about the thyroid.

**INT:** You mentioned privacy. What about sharing with a roommate or family?

**P5:** Nothing about my mood goes anywhere. I want to be very clear, because there's a template in my head here and I don't fit it. Sertraline is for anxiety, not depression. I don't have a depression diagnosis, I've never had one, and if a product writes "depression" about me it is both wrong and dangerous in a way I'll have to explain to my insurer. So: my medication list can be visible, and my *behaviour* around it is not. I don't want a partner to know that I skipped, or that I skipped twice, or that I was anxious last Tuesday. I don't want the record read as a mood. That's the line for me: the fact that I take it — fine. What it means about me — not yours.

**INT:** That's a distinction worth pausing on.

**P5:** It is, and most products don't make it, and it's the reason I'd never put my meds in one. Also the anxiety thing — I have good days and bad days and the product's job is to not be a mood ring. If my week is bad, the app should not be cheerful. I don't want "great job staying on track!" from a system on a Tuesday. Silence is better. Or just the state. State is enough.

**INT:** Notifications.

**P5:** I have exactly one medication notification and it's at 07:20 and it repeats until done. Everything else is off. If an app wants to send me a weekly report, a tip, a refill nudge, an encouragement, a "did you know" — I will not download it. Five channels maximum in this life, and it isn't one of them.

**INT:** What about a record? You've been keeping one in a sense.

**P5:** I've kept a log since 2019. A spreadsheet. Not because I'm a better person but because I wanted data and no app would let me have it. I want a *log* — dated rows, exportable, mine. If my app has a lock screen saying I've taken it, great. If it has a history I can read and get out of it as text, better. I don't want a streak, I don't want a percentage, and I don't want a chart with a smiley face. If I want to feel bad about Tuesday I already have a spreadsheet.

**INT:** Anything that would make you actually recommend something?

**P5:** The two that travel, and a plain answer about the thyroid. That's it. Take the two in the drawer and the two in the box and make them travel, and put the four sentences about the thyroid in a place I can see. And don't ask me to sign up. I have 200 accounts. I will not make the two hundred and first to swallow a pill. That's the whole review. [laughs]

**INT:** Last thing. If you saw this pitch, what would you worry about?

**P5:** That it treats me as the same person as everyone else, and that it assumes a home with a kitchen in it. The second assumption breaks the first. Every health product I have ever used has quietly assumed I'm in my own kitchen with a routine and a person who loves me. The design question isn't "how do we get her to take the pill", it's "how does the system behave when the routine is gone." That's it. That's the whole field, and it's more interesting than a checkbox.
