## Transcript

**INT:** Before we start, can I ask what you'd like to get out of this? You volunteered, so I'd like to know why.

**P3:** Because my daughter has opinions about me and my medicine. I'd like her to have less to have opinions about. [laughs] That sounds bad said out loud. No, honestly — I manage five things, I live alone, and I am not going to be dragged. If someone gives me something that works with how my brain works, fine. If it's a to-do list with medicine on it, no thank you.

**INT:** Show me how you do it now. Don't explain, just do it.

**P3:** [stage direction, observer: she crosses two names off the whiteboard on the fridge, then drops a sheet of paper into a bin bag] Right, this is my thing. I get up, I take the two that are the white ones, and then I do a tick, and I put the tick in the bin. That's it. The bin is the point.

**INT:** The bin is the point. Say more.

**P3:** Because the tick means nothing. A tick on a board is a promise to yourself and you can lie to yourself. But the bin — the bin is done. It's in the room. I can't un-drop it. I know the sheet is in the bin because I dropped it in the bin.

**INT:** And the whiteboard is the list?

**P3:** The board is for my brain so I don't have to hold it. It's not the record, it's the reminder. There's a difference and I get annoyed when people don't see the difference. Everyone confuses a reminder with a record. The board is a reminder. The bin is the record.

**INT:** This whiteboard — is it on your list of medicines, with the days?

**P3:** No, no. I don't do days. I couldn't do days. I tried it with a day-of-the-week one and it was impossible, because then if I miss a day, the day doesn't get done, ever, it just stays there being a day I failed. And I don't want a wall of undoned days. So I just do: morning, two of them. Evening, three. Every day. No days. I asked the pharmacist and he said, "But you need to know if you miss one." I said, I know if I miss one. I notice. I notice immediately.

**INT:** What about opening the bottles? Can you open them?

**P3:** [long pause] That's the real problem, isn't it. Some of them — the bone one, and the new ones from the hospital — they're sealed like a child would not open them. It takes me two hands and my whole attention and I have to put the phone down. I use a rubber band, a rubber band round the lid and I pull it. That works. A rubber band works.

**INT:** Anything else? Reading?

**P3:** I have trouble with the middle of my vision. I have a spot, and it's getting worse. I'm not going to call it by its name because I don't want to be that person in the interview. But small print — I can't. I have a magnifier app that I use, I hold the phone at the right distance, that's fine, that's what it's for. The problem is not reading. The problem is when something is too small, I don't know it's too small until I've already failed. There's no warning. A button that's too small just isn't there, as far as I'm concerned.

**INT:** Is that a good description?

**P3:** That's a very good description. I'll take that.

**INT:** If something is too small, what happens?

**P3:** I press the air. I press where the screen is, and nothing happens, or worse — something else happens. Once I pressed the wrong thing and it closed everything and I lost what I'd typed. I lost four names. That's when I stopped using that app. I didn't want to be told that I'd done it wrong, I wanted my four names back.

**INT:** Four names?

**P3:** I was entering a list for the pharmacy. Four names, all of them gone. That's when I stopped. [pause] And the thing I want to say is that nobody would be angry with me. It's the app's fault, not mine. But it made me feel like the fault was mine, because it was my finger. It's always my finger. "My finger did it" is what you hear. The app never did it. My finger did it.

**INT:** And this is a phone, so it goes everywhere, and the margins are small.

**P3:** The margins are small! That's it exactly. On a page you have room. On a phone, everything is squeezed to the edge and there's no white space and there's no room to be wrong. Give me a bit of room. A big area where nothing happens, so if I miss, I miss on nothing.

**INT:** Family. Your daughter — what does she do?

**P3:** She rings. She's very helpful and I love her and she rings to ask if I've taken them. Every day. And I'm a grown woman, and every day. Now, is she wrong to ask? No. I'd ask. But I don't want to be asked by someone who doesn't have to do it. I want to be asked by something that is not disappointed in me. I'd rather have a thing that says "you haven't ticked today" and no tone at all. No tone. I'd like a machine with no feelings.

**INT:** Would you ever let her see a record?

**P3:** Only what I choose. And here's the thing — if she sees everything, then it's a report card, and I'm a child. If she sees a summary, like a week, that's different. A week is a conversation. A day is an accusation. One of those two, not the other. And I would want to know that she knows I chose it, not that she just has access.

**INT:** The last time you missed a dose, what happened?

**P3:** I went to the doctor — the doctor or the pharmacy, one of them — and they said it wasn't a disaster. And I cried in the car, because I'd been so frightened. Just in the car for a minute. Nobody had told me that missing one wouldn't ruin me, and I'd been carrying it for two weeks. I had been afraid of my own medicine for two weeks because nobody told me the scale of a single miss.

**INT:** That's a very strong thing to say.

**P3:** It's a true thing. Nobody tells you the scale. All the leaflets are about what will go wrong. Nothing tells you what will not.

**INT:** What do you want to know, if you could ask anything?

**P3:** Is this one dangerous if I miss it? Which of my five is that one? Because I have a feeling that they are not the same, and nobody has ever told me. They all look like little white discs to me. They're not the same and I treat them the same. And if I knew one of them was the dangerous one, I would — yes, I would put a different mark on that one. A red dot. I'd move it to its own place. I'd take it out of the group.

**INT:** So you'd take one out of the routine to protect it?

**P3:** I'd take the important one out of the routine and put it somewhere that isn't the noise. Yes. Even if it means one more thing. Even if that's one more thing to remember, because at least that one would be remembered for the right reason.

**INT:** Anything about the leaflets, the boxes?

**P3:** They're written for a doctor. "Take one tablet by mouth." By mouth! I know it goes in my mouth. It's the rest I need. "Take one tablet as required for pain" — required by whom? By what? Just tell me in one sentence what it's for and what to watch for, in big letters, in English. I have a good education but that doesn't help me with a leaflet.

**INT:** Would you want to record anything yourself?

**P3:** No. I have the bin. The bin is the record and it works. If you want to know, ask the bin. [laughs] But a thing I would like — if it can't do it for me, at least it should not nag me. It should not be brave with me. It should be dull. Dull and correct.

## Analyst notes (not verbatim, added by interviewer)
- Eye condition is stated but deliberately unnamed by her. Do not invent a clinical label. Her screener says "the sugar one" and that phrase is not converted into a diagnosis either — see README trap T4.
- She names no medication at all, and never links a pill to a condition. Do not map her five screener conditions onto her five medications.
- The bin, not the whiteboard, is the record. Any design that asks her to tick boxes is a design that adds work she has already solved.
- "A week is a conversation, a day is an accusation" is the single most quotable line in the corpus. It is hers.
- The losing-four-names moment is the true breaking point, not the physical strength issue.
- She explicitly rejects tone. The persona should not be written as "anxious elder"; she rejects that framing in the interview.
- Being checked on daily is welcome. Being checked on daily *by someone who doesn't have to do it, with a tone* is not. Do not write her as surveilled.
