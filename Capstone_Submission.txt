Doseday Capstone Submission

PROJECT THEME
DoseDay — an accessible medication companion for adults managing multiple prescriptions.

FIVE-SENTENCE PITCH
1. DoseDay is for Rowan, a 59-year-old adult who works rotating shifts and manages five prescriptions — three scheduled and two taken as needed — at an employer the research corpus deliberately leaves unnamed.
2. They struggle to keep a consistent routine because doses land across changing workdays, one bad night can turn an app into a judgement, and paper organizers are easy to misplace.
3. The product is a mobile-first medication companion that turns each dose into one calm daily action, supports quick reversible check-ins, and shares a privacy-conscious weekly summary with a chosen caregiver.
4. In a four-week pilot with 30 adults managing three or more prescriptions, success means at least 80% of scheduled doses are marked "Taken" and the median first-time setup time is under three minutes.
5. I'm pursuing DoseDay because it combines accessible behavior design with a high-stakes daily habit, letting me build a useful, testable product rather than a one-off screen.

RESEARCH SYNTHESIS
- World Health Organization, 2003: adherence in developed countries averages about 50% for chronic-disease medicines.
  https://www.who.int/news/item/01-07-2003-failure-to-take-prescribed-medicine-for-chronic-diseases-is-a-massive-world-wide-problem
- CDC, 2017: barriers include complex dosing, side effects, cost, poor care coordination, and limited patient engagement.
  https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/grand-rounds/pp/2017/20170221-medication-adherence.html
- Older-adult medication-app review: common usability failures include difficult navigation, poor visibility, inflexible reminders, and too much manual entry.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5694345/
- Design response: simplify the daily view, use concrete labels with familiar icons, make check-in reversible, support flexible schedules, and never shame a missed dose.

PRIMARY USER
Rowan (participant P1), 59
Rotating shift work, employer unnamed | Five medications: three scheduled, two as-needed | Gender not stated, so written neutral (they/them)
Note: an earlier draft of this file named this user "Leila, 58, shift supervisor, hypertension and type 2 diabetes". None of that was supported by the transcript: the name, the job title, the industry and the diagnosis were all unsourced. See C-05 and C-06 in the verification log.
Goals: take the right dose on time, understand side effects, preserve privacy, share only what they choose.
Frustrations: changing shifts, overlapping reminders, tiny controls, uncertain refill timing, paper organizers that get lost.
Context: often checks their phone between work tasks; one-handed use; moderate vision and fine-motor needs.

PERSONA PACK AND VERIFICATION
Source: `interviews/` — 5 semi-structured interview transcripts, P1-P5.
IMPORTANT: this corpus is SIMULATED. No human was interviewed; it was written as a labelled practice dataset so the AI-persona pipeline could be built and tested end to end. Every board carries a SIMULATED DATA marker, and `interviews/00_README_DATA_PROVENANCE.md` states plainly that it must not be presented as fieldwork. When real interviews exist, the folder is replaced wholesale and the pack is regenerated.

Three personas, one of them an explicit merge:
- The Shifter (P1, 59, "Rowan") — rotating shift work. Fails because the app judged them; one missed night became a red circle and they deleted a good app.
- The Unbothered (P2, 34, "Jay") — warehouse team lead, stated by P2. Fails because a record becomes a judgement: "a dose log makes me a worse person."
- The Unanchored (P3+P4, 67 and 45, "Ada" and "Ray") — merged on one shared failure: a routine that lives somewhere a routine cannot follow. Labelled as the pack's weakest inference, never tested with either participant alone.
- The Audit Trail (P5, 29) — a design-literate critic kept beside the personas, not a fourth persona.

What this pack does not claim: no market size, no willingness to pay, no clinical outcomes, no adherence statistics (every rate in the source is hedged and stays hedged), and nothing about a person without a smartphone, because no participant represented that case.

CORE PROBLEM
People managing several prescriptions need one dependable daily routine, not another complicated health dashboard.

SUCCESS MEASUREMENT
Pilot: 30 adults, 4 weeks.
Primary target: at least 80% of scheduled doses marked Taken.
Setup target: median first-time setup under 3 minutes.
Guardrail: no dose decision overrides the prescription entered with the user or clinician.
