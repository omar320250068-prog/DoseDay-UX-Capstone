Doseday Capstone Submission

PROJECT THEME
DoseDay — an accessible medication companion for adults managing multiple prescriptions.

FIVE-SENTENCE PITCH
1. DoseDay is for Rowan, a 59-year-old adult who works rotating shifts and manages five prescriptions — three scheduled and two taken as needed — at an employer the research corpus deliberately leaves unnamed.
2. They struggle to keep a consistent routine because doses land across changing workdays, one bad night can turn an app into a judgement, and paper organizers are easy to misplace.
3. The product is a mobile-first medication companion that turns each dose into one calm daily action, supports quick reversible check-ins, and shares a privacy-conscious weekly summary with a chosen caregiver.
4. In a four-week pilot with 30 adults managing three or more prescriptions, success means at least 80% of scheduled doses are marked "Taken" and the median first-time setup time is under three minutes.
5. I'm pursuing DoseDay because it combines accessible behavior design with a high-stakes daily habit, letting me build a useful, testable product rather than a one-off screen.

DESK RESEARCH — 8 SOURCES, 22 REAL USER REVIEWS
Collected 2026-09-26. Every review is quoted with username and date. Vendor-selected testimonials

Competitor and analog products (3 captured, screenshotted):
- Medisafe (direct, freemium) — 4.7/5, ~100K ratings. Flow: add medication, schedule dose times,
  push reminder, mark taken. Also sells HealthKit sync, a shareable PDF for a doctor, and
  "family interaction" for a whole family on one account.
  https://apps.apple.com/us/app/medisafe-medication-management/id573916946
- Meds & Pill Reminder MyTherapy (direct, freemium) — 4.5/5, 237,756 ratings. Flow: add
  medication, set reminder, confirm or skip, see streaks. Advertises "Motivation with Streaks".
  https://apps.apple.com/us/app/pill-reminder-mytherapy/id662170995
- Hero: Medication Manager (hardware + subscription, $29.99/month) — flow: install, activate
  subscription, set schedule, physically load a 90-day supply; the dispenser sorts, locks and
  dispenses only the correct dose. Their own copy: the app "does not work as a stand-alone
  application". https://apps.apple.com/us/app/hero-medication-manager/id1352848484
- Analog: the physical pill organizer and talking pill bottle, evidenced through testimony about
  what users relied on before the app (melanie605, App Store 11/17/2019; Linda Smith, Google Play
  31 July 2026 — filling med boxes "used to take over an hour, always had an error").

Articles and reports (4, all link-checked HTTP 200):
- WHO 2003 — adherence in developed countries averages about 50% for chronic-disease medicines.
  https://www.who.int/news/item/01-07-2003-failure-to-take-prescribed-medicine-for-chronic-diseases-is-a-massive-world-wide-problem
- CDC 2017 Grand Rounds — barriers are multi-layered: complex dosing, side effects, cost, poor
  coordination, low engagement.
  https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/grand-rounds/pp/2017/20170221-medication-adherence.html
- PMC5694345 — common usability failures in older-adult medication apps: difficult navigation,
  poor visibility, inflexible reminders, too much manual entry.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5694345/
- Frontiers in Medical Technology 2021 — independently scored 8 medication apps. Every app scored
  full marks on "provides a reminder function", so reminding differentiates nobody; the category's
  security and privacy disclosure scored poorly across the board.
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medical-technology/articles/10.3389/fmedt.2020.616242/full

Real user reviews (22): Apple App Store 20, Google Play 1, Reddit 1. Positive 12, negative 10.
Ten of the twenty-two sit on the category leader, which is itself a finding.

Top 5 findings, each tied to a named source:
1. The reminder is half the product; the record is the other half. Users are not asking to be
   nagged, they are asking to stop not knowing whether they already took something.
   GeekyDragonfly (App Store 05/04/2022) "no more guessing whether I took a medicine or not";
   Meinnm505 (May 5) "It took the guess work out of the equation"; LiinaLatrell (Jan 19);
   rlsmith1994 (Jun 9) "it doesn't go away if you don't take your meds".
2. Reminder frequency is table stakes; scheduling arithmetic is where products break. All 8 apps
   scored by A4 earned full marks for providing reminders. What breaks is a regimen that is not a
   clean daily time. SF Cavendish (11/23/2021) could not enter "1 or 2 tablets/dose" and gave up;
   MikeyBGr8 (01/19/2022) refill maths ignores frequency; GeekyDragonfly (2-hourly dosing);
   Linda Smith (Google Play) "My meds vary from 1x week to 3x/day".
3. Interface depth is an accessibility problem and users name the mechanism. tuxedoyam
   (12/25/2021): people on multiple medications "might also have some degree of brain fog or mental
   impairment either due to the condition or as a side effect of medication". andrewleesh (App Store
   SG 16/07/2020) "4 taps on tiny icons". Corroborated by A3.
4. Reminder persistence means opposite things depending on who shares the record. rlsmith1994 and
   eventidephoenix (10/10/2018) want it relentless; 123334567 (08/06/2024) describes the same
   mechanic as a fault — "My husband will mark something taken, I'll get reminders on my device …
   There's a disconnect between taken status and notifications". The variable is the number of people
   writing to one record, not the user's preference.
5. In a medication app, reliability defects are trust defects. CoastieWife74 (Mar 8), a paying
   long-time user, "Now I have new meds to take and I can't add them to the app. I even tried
   deleting some and…nothing … I'll have to." Reddit r/androidapps: notifications silently not
   firing, cause was the phone's battery settings — so from the user's side "the app is broken" and
   "the reminder never arrived" are indistinguishable.

Design response: lead with a record rather than a countdown; support variable amounts and
irregular frequency including as-needed doses logged without nagging; branch reminder escalation on
whether anyone else shares the record; no streaks, no scores, no red-for-missed state; never allow a
medication to be blocked from being added.

CORE PROBLEM
People managing several prescriptions need one dependable daily routine, not another complicated health dashboard.

SUCCESS MEASUREMENT
Pilot: 30 adults, 4 weeks.
Primary target: at least 80% of scheduled doses marked Taken.
Setup target: median first-time setup under 3 minutes.
Guardrail: no dose decision overrides the prescription entered with the user or clinician.

Source: `DoseDay_Page_1b_IA_flows.svg`, geometry-checked in-browser at 1920 x 4400, 277 text

Two primary users, each with a job to be done traced to named UX-02 findings:
- User 1, the solo doser: Rowan, 59, rotating shift work, five medications (three scheduled, two
  as-needed), employer unnamed, gender unstated so written neutral. JTBD: "When my shifts rotate
  and I am the only one keeping track, I want one screen that shows what is already done before it
  shows me what is next, so I can stop guessing whether I took something." Traces to F-1, F-2, F-3.
- User 2, the shared-record carer: two people writing to one medication record. JTBD: "When my
  spouse and I both look after the same routine, I want a dose marked on either phone to stop the
  reminder on the other, so I stop getting alarms for something that has already been handled."
  Traces to F-4, F-7, F-5. This user is deliberately unnamed: the corpus has no shared-household
  persona, because Ada and Ray are two separate people rather than a couple, so the archetype is
  taken from the UX-02 review thread (123334567, App Store 08/06/2024) with no name, age, job or
  diagnosis invented to fill the gap.

IA map: 25 screens in 6 groups — Start (one-time spine, S1–S7 plus empty state E1), Today
(default tab, S8–S11), Medicines (S12–S16), Progress (S17–S19), Shared care (cross-cutting, S20–S21)
and Settings (utility, S22–S25). Grouping logic is stated on the board as G1–G6 and each rule cites
the NN/g Information Architecture Study Guide: three tabs plus utility rather than a hamburger
(NN/g, Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics); two taps from Today to anything but
not flattened into one screen (NN/g, Flat vs. Deep; NN/g, The 3-Click Rule for Navigation Is False,
so S23 is allowed to sit at 3 taps); groups named by user goal rather than database object
(NN/g, Information Scent); as-needed doses handled as a category outlier by cross-listing rather
than duplication (NN/g, How to Handle Category Outliers; Polyhierarchy); and recovery placed next to
the failure rather than in a help centre (NN/g, Navigation: You Are Here). Shared care is
deliberately not a tab, because a shared-care tab would be empty for the majority of users. Stated
plainly on the board: the grouping was not validated by a card sort or tree test, because both need
participants and the evidence base is 22 self-selected reviews.

Three flows, each with decision points, an unhappy path, and the law named at the decision:
- Flow 1, primary task, "log a dose and know it is logged" (Rowan, F-1). Two decision points
  (scheduled or as-needed; right dose, right time) plus the undo-window decision. Unhappy path
  covers a wrong dose and an expired undo window, which recovers through the editable history in
  S14 rather than dead-ending. Laws: Hick's Law, Paradox of Choice, Undo, Recognition Rather Than
  Recall, Doherty Threshold, Von Restorff, Peak-End Rule.
- Flow 2, onboarding and first run (F-2, F-4). Three decision points: allow notifications, does
  anyone else write here, add the first medicine now or skip. Two unhappy paths: notifications
  refused (the product stays usable in check-in-only mode, the consequence is stated before the OS
  prompt, and the prompt is re-offered once from Today instead of as a modal) and an abandoned or
  skipped run (partial progress persists and setup resumes where it stopped; the empty state E1
  ships one worked example rather than a blank wall). Laws: Peak-End Rule, Paradox of Choice,
  Chunking, Goal-Gradient Effect, Zeigarnik Effect, Signal, Constraints, Endowment Effect, Affordance.
- Flow 3, recovery, "the reminder never arrived" (F-5). Three decision points, and the first one is
  deliberately "was this marked on another device?" because a genuine second writer is the most
  likely innocent explanation and 123334567 experienced it as a fault rather than as intended
  behaviour. Unhappy path: no cause found and the dose already late, where the dose is logged
  anyway and recorded honestly as taken late rather than shown in red. Laws: Help Users Recover
  from Errors, Signal, Recognition Rather Than Recall, Layering, Redundancy, Undo, Hick's Law,
  Doherty Threshold, Processing Fluency.
